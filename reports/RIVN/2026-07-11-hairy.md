# Hairy's bear case: Rivian (RIVN) — 2026-07-11

## The case

I want to like this story. Software-defined vehicles, a legacy OEM writing nine-figure
checks, insiders buying — it reads well as a paragraph. It reads much worse as a
balance sheet. Strip the narrative and what's left is a company whose core product
line is *shrinking*, whose margin "improvement" is rented rather than earned, and
whose quality record shows the same failure mode recurring across four different
safety systems while it tries to triple production for R2. None of that goes away
because Volkswagen wrote a check.

Start with the number that should worry everyone: automotive revenue fell 14.6% YoY
to $3.830B even as total revenue rose 8.4%. That's not a rounding error — that's
the actual car business, the thing Rivian is supposed to be scaling, going
backwards while total revenue is dressed up by $1.551B of software/services growth
(226% YoY) and a one-time-feeling $836M spike in VW payments (from $73M) plus
$197M of regulatory credits. John's own numbers show the cost of software revenue
more than doubled too, from $477M to $981M — this isn't a high-margin annuity
paying for itself, it's a cost center growing in lockstep with the revenue it
generates. Automotive gross margin at 1.03% is essentially breakeven only because
non-recurring VW and credit revenue is propping it up. Take those two line items
out and the core vehicle business is still bleeding on every unit it ships — the
same conclusion John reached independently.

The balance sheet backs up the concern rather than easing it. Cash dropped $1.715B
YoY to $3.579B. Total debt of $5.233B against that cash position means Rivian is in
a net-debt position once you strip out short-term investments. Free cash flow
burned -$1.3B TTM. Into that backdrop, management raised $1.32B by selling 75
million new shares — roughly 6% dilution at a moment the stock was already down
18% on the announcement. Yes, this protected DOE loan-guarantee covenants. But
read what that means: Rivian's cash position was tight enough that *ordinary
operations risked breaching a covenant* without an emergency equity sale. That is
what companies running low on runway do. It is not a sign of strength dressed as
prudence; John estimates 3-4 years of runway *if* burn doesn't worsen, and R2's
ramp (capex-intensive by definition) is exactly the kind of event that worsens
burn before it improves revenue.

On engineering: I pulled the same NHTSA data Baldy did, and I read it differently.
Twelve recall campaigns since May 2024 sounds manageable until you look at *what
recurs*. Four separate campaigns hit critical fasteners on safety-relevant systems:
airbag attachment clips (June 2024), seat belt front anchorage D-ring bolts (June
2025), seat belt critical fastener bolts (January 2026), and suspension critical
fasteners (January 2026). That is the same failure mode — improperly torqued or
secured fasteners — recurring across four different systems over 19 months. Two
separate headlight recalls (May 2024 and February 2025) hit within nine months of
each other, meaning the first fix didn't fully solve the underlying issue. And the
recall pace has not slowed as the company matures: four of the twelve campaigns
landed between August 2025 and January 2026 alone — roughly one every seven weeks
in the most recent stretch, right as Rivian pushes toward higher-volume R2
production. NHTSA also shows 76 consumer complaints logged for model years
2025-2026 alone — a young fleet generating complaint volume this early is not the
signature of a company that has manufacturing dialed in. Amy's sentiment data adds
a live example that hasn't hit the recall system yet: owners reporting charging
adapters pulling 600+ amps against a 500A rating — a thermal/fire-risk pattern that
looks exactly like the kind of thing that becomes recall #13.

Zoom out to the market Rivian actually has to win in. Global EV sales grew 21% in
2025 to 21.2M units — genuinely strong — but that growth is almost entirely
non-American. China hit 13.3M units (+18% YoY) and Europe hit 4.3M (+35% YoY),
while the US, Rivian's only real market today, grew a nearly flat 0.7% (1.52M to
1.53M units). Rivian doesn't get to sell into China's or Europe's growth — it's
fighting for share in the one major region where EV demand has essentially
stalled, against Tesla, GM, Ford and legacy incumbents who aren't burning $1.3B a
year to stay in the game. Layer David's macro finding on top — consumer sentiment
down 14.2% YoY — and you have an affordability-stressed buyer pool for a company
selling trucks that start well north of $70K, in the slowest-growing EV market on
the planet.

## Rebuttal (round 2)

**On "one of the largest automakers on earth paying real money, at real scale, for
Rivian's software" as third-party validation:** This treats VW's $836M as proof of
durable demand for Rivian's platform. It's proof of one contract with one customer.
Concentration risk cuts the opposite direction from Baldy's framing: Rivian now has
a single external counterparty responsible for a chunk of the swing that turned
automotive gross margin positive, and John's own data shows the cost of software
revenue rose from $477M to $981M in the same period — the segment's *profitability*
hasn't been demonstrated, only its top-line growth. Meanwhile the business VW isn't
paying for — Rivian's own vehicles — shrank 14.6%. A single customer covering for a
shrinking core business is fragility, not validation.

**On the April insider buying spike being "hard to explain as random option
exercises" and evidence insiders "bought into the dilution, not around it":**
Baldy leans hard on one data point while John — who actually ran the numbers —
called this exact signal ambiguous: *"the timing... could reflect option
exercises, hedging adjustments, or pre-disclosure position-taking. The sustained
selling in prior months is more indicative of insider skepticism about near-term
returns."* Nine months of predominantly negative MSPR readings (multiple -100
prints) is a much larger, much more consistent sample than one anomalous month.
One green month sandwiched inside three quarters of red is not a reversal signal;
it's noise Baldy is reading as narrative.

**On the recall record being "disciplined quality control learning in public...
not negligence":** Baldy is right that there's no fire or brake-failure campaign
in the record — I won't pretend otherwise, that's a real point in Rivian's favor
relative to the worst-case headline risk. But "no campaign has killed anyone yet"
is a low bar, and the pattern underneath tells a different story than "learning
fast." Four separate fastener-related recalls across airbags, seat belts (twice),
and suspension in 19 months, plus two headlight recalls nine months apart, is
evidence the *same class* of manufacturing quality-control problem (fastener
torque/attachment) keeps resurfacing on different parts of the vehicle rather than
being solved once and closed out. A company that had genuinely fixed its
torque-control process wouldn't need four bites at variations of the same defect
type. Combine that with the 76 complaints on a fleet that's barely two model-years
old and Amy's report of owners already flagging a charging-adapter over-current
issue that hasn't reached NHTSA yet, and the more honest read is: quality issues
are still emerging as fast as Rivian ramps volume, not shrinking.
